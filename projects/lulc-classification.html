Multi-temporal Land Cover Classification of Faisalabad

A Random Forest classification of Landsat imagery for 2010 and 2020 to develop land-cover map for Faisalabad district with limited reference data and to examine the main sources of classification error.

Context

Objective and reference-data challenge

The project used a supervised Landsat classification workflow to map five broad land-cover classes in Faisalabad district for 2010 and 2020 which included Tree Cover, Cropland, Built-up, Bare Surface and Water.

Reference polygons were prepared separately for each year. For 2020, samples could be checked against high-resolution imagery (Google Earth Pro) and Landsat time series. For 2010, reference data were more limited, so interpretation relied more on Landsat composites, spectral indices, seasonal patterns and historical imagery where available.

Research focus

How can Landsat imagery and limited reference data be used to produce a reliable land-cover classification and identify the main sources of classification error?

Approach

Landsat classification and error analysis

The workflow combined Landsat spectral information, seasonal indicators and manually prepared reference samples to train a Random Forest classifier and examine the main sources of classification error.

01

Preprocessing

Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 imagery with scaling, cloud, shadow and saturation masking.

02

Feature engineering

Spectral bands, land surface temperature, NDVI, MNDWI, NDBI, BSI and seasonal vegetation metrics.

03

Reference data

Year-specific polygons prepared within visually homogeneous land-cover areas.

04

Random Forest

300-tree classifier trained and evaluated using separate reference polygons.

05

Error diagnosis

Misclassified areas were examined to identify confusion between classes and improve the reference samples.

Results

2010 and 2020 land-cover classification

Cropland remained the dominant mapped class in both years, while the built-up area was larger in the 2020 classification. Changes in bare surface, water and tree cover were interpreted more cautiously because differences in reference data, river conditions and mixed pixels made direct comparison between the two years less certain.

Interpretation & limitations

What improved the model

The error analysis showed that exposed river surfaces and some bare areas were occasionally confused with built-up land in the 2010 imagery. In rural settlements, this was likely related to mud-built structures and surrounding bare soil having similar spectral responses, while mixed 30 m Landsat pixels added further uncertainty.

For 2020, some Tree Cover errors occurred in mixed 30 m pixels where cropland also contained field-boundary trees, roadside vegetation or trees along canals. Adding more representative cropland samples from these areas helped reduce the over-prediction of Tree Cover.

Historical reference information was more limited for 2010 than for 2020, and the validation was based on held-out reference polygons rather than independent ground observations. The comparison between 2010 and 2020 is therefore exploratory in nature rather than as an accurate land-cover change assessment.

Research takeaway

Most improvements came from understanding the classification errors and improving the reference data rather than making the model more complex.

Temporal wetness and soil information, together with better representation of difficult land-cover areas, helped address some of the main sources of confusion.
